To Ophie: My thoughts on the Sami Gold thing

TL;DR - I don't respect Sami Gold's recent breakdown and I don't think you should either.

(No actual offense to Sami of course. I may not respect him but I don't hate him. Think of this as ribbing rather than an assault on his character.)

Recently (as in, a couple of hours ago) I had an interesting discussion on twitter with user opheliamoding. It was regarding the posts made by Sami Gold on his newfound religious fervour. Ophie, being religious herself, was of the view that it is normal and a sign of humble piety to prostate oneself in front of the divine in the extremely public manner in which Sami was. Being an atheist, I made some jest about her tweet. That led to a sensible back and forth where I attempted to divine (heh) and explain to her what I was personally finding so annoying about Sami's weird long turn to religiosity. The following is a hurried rationalisation of my thought process in what might amount to genuinely unconscionable rambling.

In the course of our discussion, when I bought up the concept of privilege, Ophie did rightly point out (and I agree with her) that Sami has the privilege of being able to materially afford to have a religious crisis, but that wasn't what I was originally going for as a critique.

To understand what I meant, I am reproducing my framing from the discussion of how I believe a person's worldview should work: To sum up, I think that by adulthood we should all have developed a framework of how the world actually functions. If we come across new information about something, it should be able to slot into our framework of reality relatively easily, unless the information is completely counter to the foundations of our previous knowledge.

So e.g. if you learn the physics behind how a hard drive functions, it shouldn't shatter your worldview because you already knew that hard drives were a medium through which computers store information, though you might feel a new appreciation for the kind of engineering required to create hard drives. However the fundamental fact about hard drives being a data storage medium that you had in your mind hasn't shifted and you don't really need to adjust how you operate. Similarly, if you learnt about a rich person doing something heinously evil, it shouldn't be mind-blowingly impossible for you to conceive of it, because you should have internalised some kind of generalism like "wealth and power only reveal the true nature of the person wielding them", and you should have been able to remember enough historical examples of evil people (e.g. Hitler) to understand the depths of depravity that humans are capable of.

I am hung up on describing information as the source of change within the framework because that's what everything I can think of can be boiled down to. A drug trip, a stranger's kindness, the warmth of the sun.. all of these are experiences, to be sure, but they are also information that feeds into our world-view. If you experience a stranger's kindness for the first time, then your world-view shifts to include the information that strangers can be kind. And sure, we would prefer that a stranger was kind or that the sun warmed us on a cold day, but our framework of the world allows for all the events that we consider possible, not just the ones we prefer would happen. You know that it can rain, even though you wouldn't like it and would rather the sun stayed up that day.

Essentially, in my conception, a person's world-view is their accumulated information about the world at large, as well as how they feel or relate to their perception of the world as seen through the lens of that information. Someone who has never encountered kindness from strangers, and only expects the worst from them, might genuinely be shaken when they experience such kindness. To make a terrible physical analogy, think of a person's world-view as a bookshelf. Their information about the world is what forms the books inside, and their feelings about the information are the annotations and bookmarks and post-it notes and highlighter marks in a well-loved personal library. I could torture this analogy a lot further, by equating their life experiences with a memooir, or some other silly nonsense. Suffice to say everything can be represented as either information or feelings (the word "feeling" is not enough to fully describe what I'm aiming for but forgive me, this is the best I could do at 4am). Given this conception, it follows that the information and feelings contained in a person's world-view informs their behaviour and outlook towards things.

And of course, religion is a part of someone's world-view. After all, if you have a religious belief it means you take certain articles of faith to be concrete reality, forming a component of information that informs and influences your behaviour and outlook. A Christian would necessarily believe in the concept of a God, one who had created the world, and sent his son Jesus Christ to suffer for the sins of all mankind so they could all ascend to heaven. I think that's at least the minimum common denominator of what all christians believe, right? (Don't correct me if the calvinists don't think everyone is ascending to heaven or whatever such.)

I do have to note, just to be certain, that when I say information, I do NOT assert whether it is true or not. Someone might believe a piece of information to be true, such as the existence of God or the unkindness of strangers, without it being true for anyone else. The factual truthfulness of information is entirely divorced from whether it is true in a person's world-view, the framework through which they perceive reality.

Now, the only reason that a piece of information such as, say, for a random example, "Jeffrey Epstein was a major political power broker who provided powerful pedophiles with access to children, probably in return for their fealty to Israel" would completely destroy a person's world-view, would be if their framework of reality had been incorrectly formed in the first place. Maybe they didn't think pedophilia was real, maybe they didn't think it was possible for a bunch of rich people to be so casually evil; any of the pieces of information presented in that statement, if contra to what a person believed true about the world, might cause a personal crisis.

In this I will admit that if e.g. tomorrow a creature claiming to be the Christian God appeared in the skies above the planet and we were able to prove that it was indeed one and the same as it claimed and that everything some sect of christianity said was true, then I would be forced to change a major chunk of my atheistic beliefs about the world. That would be because I had received piece of information new to me, that was completely contrary to most other pieces of information I had received growing up and which I used to form my world-view.

That would constitute a religious crisis.

I hope you see where I am going with this.

To sum up so far - I assert that for an adult to have a religious crisis, it means that they have received or perceived some brand new piece of information- whether through a personal experience, or through learning some kind of fact about the world- that upends their belief system which had incorporated religious elements. They have received information that they cannot reconcile with the existing framework, particularly the parts that are based on the religious beliefs that they hold to be true, that they use to perceive the world. We can hopefully agree that this is what constitutes a religious crisis, yes?

Now, let's assume no earth-shattering discoveries or relevations have occurred in the general realm, no sudden and irrefutable appearances of various religious deities that would force everyone on the planet to reckon with a reshaping of the facts.

In that case, where nothing has fundamentally changed in the world, I consider two mechanisms possible for your world view to be so wrong that learning something that at least some other people already knew would cause you to have a crisis of faith.

The first would be if you grew up sheltered from the world at large. If say, you lived your entire life in a cult like scientology, with limited access to outside sources of information. Then it would be understandable that if you ever managed to receive information about how wrong your world-view from childhood was, you would have a massive crisis of faith even if the world at large hadn't changed. Note that in this example we pre-suppose that the scientologist has somehow been convinced to actually accept the truth of information that reconfigures their world-view. We'll only count information here as being received by someone if they truly do believe that it is true. Otherwise, we are bombarded by information all the time, most of which we dismiss as noise and filter out.

The second mechanism by which someone would have a crisis of faith without the world itself changing suddenly, is if someone were ignorant of aspects of reality - but unlike the previous case, this ignorance would have to be substantial despite sufficient resources and opportunities to correct. I'll give a low-stakes example - it would be unlikely for an American child born to a middle class family in the 2010s to have the occasion to use a rotary landline telephone. Yet if they so desired, they could search up on the internet about how such phones worked, how to operate them and so on... Their ignorance about the operation of a rotary landline phone is not because there's no opportunity for them to learn, but because they chose not to. And in this example, it makes sense for a kid to choose not to fall down a wikipedia rabbit hole about pulse dialling. It's also not likely that if such a kid eventually learnt about rotary encoders or pulse dialling, they would have a breakdown of their worldview.

Now, let's scale up. Imagine you are an elderly boomer. You have trawled enough facebook for you to have read and been convinced by anti-vaxx anecdotes on the internet, despite both your lived experience of the 20th century and enough of the scientific community and world organisations telling you that vaccines do not cause autism, along with a plethora of actual scientific data that you can easily access, telling you the same. Choosing then to be anti-vaxx is a deliberate choice to ignore a bunch of evidence and instead believe in unscientific anecdotes pushed by a bunch of psychopaths. If some of these people later eventually become convinced that they had been wrong, then the revelation would create a crisis of faith in themselves. Sure there is broad interplay of factors at work here and obviously disinformation originates with malicious intent, but ultimately there is some quantum of blame on the people who choose to believe disinformation despite having access to the actual facts. You could replace anti-vaxxers with flat-earthers, moon-hoaxers, and climate change deniers, and ultimately the fact would be that there are people who should know better, had the opportunity to know better and yet essentially refused to face reality. Such people will either die with their delusions or eventually have to face their cognitive dissonance, and face a crisis of whatever faith they held that pushed them to hold those beliefs in the first place.

(I'm sorry this is so long-winded, if I had more time I would edit it down to a reasonable length)

What I'm trying to get at is that the mechanism through which someone would have a crisis of faith is either:

  • they never had access to vast swathes of information about the world and thus don't know, meaning contact with some piece of information could unravel everything they thought they knew previously; or
  • they had relatively easy access to resources and information and yet chose to be uninformed about something so massive that it blew their world-view to smithereens.

I believe every case of somone having a crisis of faith can be boiled down to ignorance stemming from one of these two situations. And I think, from the way I've framed it, that you can guess what I feel about each of the two paths to ignorance.

To be clear on this as well, I don't find ignorance itself to be abhorrent. Of course we cannot be cognisant of every topic in the world, that's not my point at all. What I'm trying to say is that the way we reach and frame our information of reality is important. There is a border between accidental and wilful ignorance, and the latter is harmful to allow civilisationally. Every sentiment of anti-intellectualism comes from people who are wilfully ignorant about certain things, whether it be vaccines or trans rights or climate change.

Coming finally to our topic, Sami Gold is a Jewish citizen of the United States of America. He seems to be a columnist for some magazine or other so I have to assume he has at least had an education. I don't know anything else about this man, other than his generally left-of-centre political outlook on twitter, and his recent turn to religious fervour on the same platform. I don't even know what flavour of abrahamic deity Sami is praying to, and I don't think it matters that much to my point anyway.

What does matter is that apparently Sami is having a crisis of faith, a moment of religious doubt. What exactly about? Matters not. It's his personal affair. We are concerned merely with the fact that he's expressed having one at all. Why? From the framework we've built up so far, we can assume that he (relatively recently) got a piece of information that changed his entire outlook. He's an educated caucasian male living in the US (his pinned tweet calls New York home), a demographic that has more privileged access to information and resources about the world than literally any other in the entirety of human history. If he's having a crisis of faith then the only way the information that caused it is new to him and yet substantial enough to cause a crisis in the first place is if he chose to remain ignorant to it. It may be my ignorant third world upbringing speaking but I genuinely cannot imagine a scenario where some degree of deliberate ignorance is not the case for this crisis. And so, far from humble piety, I only see Sami's moment of doubt as the grapplings of a dumbass.

Though, I don't think Sami should worry too much. Better to be a dumbass than an outright fascist.
